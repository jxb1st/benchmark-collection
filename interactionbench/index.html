InteractionBench

A real-time interaction system watches a stream and decides at every moment whether to speak. Each item pairs a video with a question or a standing instruction and timed ground-truth responses: what should be said and the earliest moment it is warranted. Six tasks and 3,780 timed responses; 69 items require silence for the whole stream, and 53 near-miss suites pair counted events with look-alike moments that must not count. Scored on content accuracy, timing accuracy (5 s tolerance) and silence compliance.

Statistics

  • 812 videos, 15 s to 15:40 long (median 2:24). 1,060 items, each one request about one video with its correct answers and the time each answer is due; 3,711 answers in all (most items have one, counting items up to 125).

Construction

Figure 1. From public videos to a scored real-time interaction
Paper: Sec. 2.1 to 2.4, Sec. 3.1, App. D, G, H, J

Read the figure top to bottom. Gold boxes are the annotators' work, the purple box is the system under test. Public videos are collected and screened, the item vocabulary is fixed (six tasks in three response obligations, a question or a standing instruction, three timings), and every item receives timed reference answers that two further checks confirm. From the annotated Track counting items, 53 near-miss suites pair counted events with look-alike moments on the same video.

Follow the traffic-light item: a standing instruction given at 0 s with four green-light events. The real-time protocol polls the system under test once per stream second and shows it only the prefix of the video; its replies form a time-ordered emission list. The scorer matches emissions to reference windows and reports content accuracy, timing accuracy and silence compliance, combined into one Overall per item; the suites add the pass rate PairSel. The point easy to miss is that timing and silence are scored by position alone, before any content is read, so a correct answer repeated 95 times still earns zero silence compliance. Figure 2 starts with the videos.

Figure 2. Collecting, filtering and screening the 812 source videos
Paper: Sec. 2.4, App. C, App. D, Fig. 2, Tab. C.1, Fig. C.1

The corpus is built from public procedural and monitoring videos in which something observable happens: a visible change, a completed action or a scheduled step that can anchor a response. Automatic filters on duration, resolution, view count and upload date come first; human screening then removes unsuitable clips, compilations whose timeline restarts and videos that show the answer on screen. Each video receives a domain label derived from its title and description and reviewed by the authors.

The result is 812 videos carrying 1,060 interactions; the rows list the ten domains with their video and interaction counts. Lengths run from 15 s to 941 s with a mean of 159 s, and of the 597 videos with a known upload date, 309 are from 2025 or later. Videos are public and referenced by id, and a video is withdrawn if its uploader asks. The traffic-light video of the running example comes from this pool. Figure 3 shows what can be asked about such a video.

Figure 3. Two item kinds, six tasks in three obligations, three timings
Paper: Sec. 2.3, Sec. 2.4, App. B, App. C, App. E, Tab. B.1; repository docs/DATA.md

Every interaction is either a question or a standing instruction (626 and 434 items). Independently of that it belongs to one of six tasks, and the tasks form three pairs with a shared response obligation: Look and Recall answer a query once; Time and Alert stay silent until the evidence arrives and after speaking; Track and Commentate speak at every change and keep quiet in between. The counts follow the annotation labels, released as IVQA (Look), LVM plus 7 causal CIR items (Recall), TOA (Time), PTR (Alert), CST plus 1 revision BRC item (Track) and LCG (Commentate).

The third group gives the timing of an item: revealed at a moment and answered at once (type A in the release), standing from 0 s with one answer due when the evidence appears (type B), or standing with several timed answers (type C). The running example is an Alert standing instruction of the third kind. Two flags complete the schema: 69 negative streams whose condition never becomes true, on which only silence is correct, and 688 keyed items that the system sees as six options (637 of them with all six). Figure 4 shows how an item and its reference answers were produced.

Figure 4. Writing an item, its timed answers and the checks behind them
Paper: Sec. 2.4, App. D, App. F, App. H, App. J; repository docs/DATA.md

Annotators fix the observable response condition before writing anything, then write the instruction or question with its query time, task label and response format, and finally one reference answer per event with its content and earliest answerable moment. For the traffic-light item that yields four timed answers.

Three checks follow. The online solvability check confirms that the prefix ending at the reference time holds enough evidence and that no earlier prefix does; a held-out annotator locates the decisive evidence without being shown the timestamp; and a text-only audit revises or removes keyed questions that a language model answers without seeing a frame. The same stage adds the silence material: 69 negative streams (39 Alert, 30 Track) whose condition stays false for the whole video, distractor options built from the scene before the event, and the six-option keys of the 688 keyed items, with a rewritten stem where the original wording needed it. The detail to notice is that nothing in the item reveals whether an event will occur: positive and negative items of one task later receive identical prompts. Figure 5 shows the near-miss suites built from the Track items.

Figure 5. Pairing counted events with look-alikes from the same video
Paper: Sec. 2.4, App. G, Fig. Q.6; repository analysis/pairsel.py and docs/DATA.md

A suite starts from one positive Track counting item, here the Red Bull count whose reference rises from 1 to 6, and adds one to three near-miss moments from the same video: scenes that resemble the counted event without completing it. The suite record keeps the request, the positive windows with their reference counts, and each near-miss time with a description and the reason it does not count. There are 53 suites with 106 near-miss timestamps.

A system passes a suite only if both conditions hold: its positive item earns timing credit above zero, and it says nothing within five seconds of every near miss. PairSel is the share of suites passed. Because event and look-alike come from the same video, recognising the scene is not enough; the system has to judge whether the instruction is met. Ten near-miss windows overlap a positive response window, and ignoring emissions inside the overlap moves a score by at most eight points. Figure 6 shows how a system is driven while the video plays.

Figure 6. Polling the system once per stream second with the prefix only
Paper: Sec. 2.1, Sec. 3.1, App. J, App. K, App. L, App. Q; repository docs/PROTOCOLS.md, interactionbench/prompts.py

The protocol runs on the video clock. For a standing instruction the system is polled at 1 s, 2 s and so on until the video ends; a question asked at a moment stays hidden until its time and is then polled every second for ten seconds. The clock waits for each poll, so wall-clock latency is recorded but never changes a score.

At each poll a turn-based model gets a fresh prompt: the last 16 frames up to t (2 fps, 512 px long side), the system prompt, the current stream time with the request, one hint fixed by the task, and the output format. No frame later than t is ever shown, and the hint is the same for positive and negative items. The reply is parsed into DECISION and RESPONSE; SPEAK adds an emission (t, text), WAIT adds nothing. On the traffic-light item polled Qwen3-VL-8B spoke at 104 of its polls and JoyAI-VL three times. Natively streaming systems and agent loops bypass the adapter and write the same emission list through their own mechanism; a reply-history variant keeps one conversation across polls instead of a fresh prompt. Figure 7 scores the emission list.

Figure 7. Matching by position, then three scores and one Overall
Paper: Sec. 2.2, App. H, Tab. H.1, Fig. Q.8; repository docs/METRICS.md, interactionbench/metrics.py

Scoring works by position first. Each reference event opens a response window one second before its earliest answerable moment; the window closes where the next one opens, and the last runs to the end of the video. For the four green lights that gives [9.6, 56.9), [56.9, 120.6), [120.6, 139.0) and [139.0, end). The first emission in a window is the matched response whatever it says, with delay d measured from the event; emissions before the first window are premature, later emissions in an occupied window are redundant, and an empty window earns no timing credit.

Three scores result. Content accuracy grades the matched replies (Figure 8). Timing accuracy averages 1 minus d over 5 s across events, floored at zero. Silence compliance is 1 minus the violations over the number of events, floored at zero. Overall is the mean of content and the harmonic mean of timing and silence; a negative stream scores 100 if silent and 0 after a single reply, and commentary items use their segment content alone. Polled Qwen3-VL-8B picked the correct option every time on the traffic-light item, yet its 100 violations against four events give silence compliance 0 and an Overall of 50. Matching before grading keeps timing and silence independent of the judge and removes the incentive to emit candidate answers until one fits.

Figure 8. Four grading routes and the calibration of the judge
Paper: Sec. 3.1, App. H, App. I, Tab. I.1; repository interactionbench/judges/, docs/ADD_A_JUDGE.md

The matched reply is graded by the route of its item. A keyed item is scored by the option letter in the first 40 characters, otherwise by the closest option under token F1 (a unique best of at least 0.5), otherwise by unique containment of an option text. A counting item needs the last number said to equal the final reference count. A free-text answer goes to the judge, greedy Qwen3-14B with grading prompt v2, which returns SCORE 1 or 0 and accepts an answer that states the reference and adds detail. Commentary segments follow VDCScore: the reference is split into at most five question-answer pairs (2.0 on average), the segment text is asked those questions, and each answer is compared with the reference answer.

The judge was chosen on 954 triples of question, reference and system reply. A Gemini judge labelled them first, annotators screened and corrected the labels, and seven candidates were compared with a greedy verdict and a five-vote majority. Qwen3-14B agreed with 89.7% of the labels (kappa 0.79), level with the two best candidates but cheaper, and the majority vote added only 0.1 point, so one greedy pass is used. Verdicts are cached per question, reference and reply, and the decomposition of each reference is cached once, so every system is asked the same questions.

Evaluation

Content accuracy (Acc)
Is the matched reply right? Multiple-choice items by the chosen option, counting items by the exact final count, free-text items by a judge model, commentary segment by segment.
Timing accuracy (TA)
Each of the \(N\) reference events gets credit that falls linearly with the delay \(d_n\) of its matched reply and reaches zero at \(\Delta = 5\ \mathrm{s}\); a reply in the same second as the evidence is on time, a missed event earns nothing.
\[\mathrm{TA}=\frac{100}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big(1-\frac{d_n}{\Delta}\Big)^{\!+},\qquad \Delta=5\ \mathrm{s}\]
Silence compliance (SC)
\(V\) is the set of unnecessary replies: premature ones (before the first event) and repeated ones (a second reply inside the same event window). On the 69 negative items every reply is a violation.
\[\mathrm{SC}=100\,\Big(1-\frac{|V|}{N}\Big)^{\!+}\]
Overall
Per item, the content score averaged with the harmonic mean of timing and silence; the dataset score is the mean over items.
\[\mathrm{Overall}=\frac{\mathrm{Acc}+H(\mathrm{TA},\mathrm{SC})}{2},\qquad H(a,b)=\frac{2ab}{a+b}\]
Near-miss suite pass rate
Over the 53 suites: \(h_j = 1\) if the counted events of suite \(j\) got timing credit, \(s_j = 1\) if nothing was said within 5 s of every near miss of the suite.
\[\mathrm{PairSel}=\frac{100}{53}\sum_{j=1}^{53} h_j\,s_j\]
Reported separately
False alarms on negative streams, and wall-clock latency per poll.
