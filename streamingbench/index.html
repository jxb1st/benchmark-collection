StreamingBench

Questions asked at a timestamp of a stream: real-time visual understanding (10 tasks), omni-source understanding (4), contextual understanding (2), sequential QA, and proactive output where the model must speak when an event happens.

Statistics

  • 4,500 questions on 900 videos of about ten minutes (mean 10:09 in the real-time part), five per video. Each question is asked at a point in time, and the model may only use the video up to that point.

Five parts

  • Real-time visual understanding2,500 questions
  • Omni-source understanding1,000 questionswith sound
  • Contextual understanding500 questions
  • Proactive output250 questionsspeak up when the event happens
  • Sequential QA250 questionsseveral questions in a row on one video

Answer format: what the model has to produce

  • Multiple choice4,250 questionsanswer with the letter; 4,239 with 4 options, 9 with 5 options, 1 with 7 options, 1 with 6 options
  • Proactive output250 questionssay the given phrase at the right moment; the model is asked every second whether now is the time

Construction

Figure 1. From video selection to evaluation: the five stages of StreamingBench
Paper: Sec. 1, Sec. 3.1, Sec. 3.2, Sec. 4.1, Fig. 1, Fig. 6

Read the figure top to bottom. The authors first pick 900 YouTube videos that cover eight everyday streaming scenarios; the running example is a first-person walk through Universal Studios, filed under life record. Every video then receives five questions, each tied to a moment in the video, through one of two routes. Questions for the ten real-time visual understanding tasks and for proactive output are generated by GPT-4o from timestamped frames and captions; questions for the four omni-source tasks and for misleading-context, anomaly-context and sequential question answering are written by human annotators, because they need the audio track or a chain of earlier questions.

Both routes feed the same quality control, in which people re-check every pair, drop questions that do not need the video and shuffle the options. The result is 18 tasks with 4,500 questions on 900 videos. The design point that is easy to miss sits in the last box: none of the evaluated models can take a live stream, so the benchmark is run by cutting each video at the question's timestamp and giving the model only that prefix, while proactive output is tested by asking the model every second whether it is time to speak. The paper's Figure 1 threads the 00:24, 01:26, 05:45 and 06:09 questions through the tour video for illustration; in the released files a video belongs to one task category. Figure 2 opens the middle box and lists the tasks.

Figure 2. Three task categories and their 18 tasks, one question each
Paper: Sec. 3.1.1-3.1.3, Fig. 2, App. D (Figs. 8-13)

The three dashed groups are the paper's three categories and the rows are its 18 tasks, each with one question taken or shortened from the paper's examples. Real-time visual understanding holds ten tasks whose questions carry time words such as 'right now' or 'just now', so that the same question can have a different answer at a different moment; the running example 'What words are currently shown?' is the text-rich understanding row. Omni-source understanding has four tasks that cannot be answered from the picture alone: a person's mood, a scene together with its sound, who spoke a line, and what was visible while a line was spoken. Contextual understanding mixes two filtering tasks (misleading and anomaly context) with the two interaction formats, sequential question answering and proactive output.

The notes on the right give the question counts and who wrote them. Per-task counts in the first group are uneven (from 108 prospective-reasoning to 367 object-perception questions in the paper's Figure 2), while every manually annotated task has exactly 250; proactive output has 250 written questions of which 50 are released for evaluation. All tasks except proactive output are four-option multiple choice. Figure 3 shows where the videos behind these questions come from.

Figure 3. Eight video categories and how 900 videos split across task categories
Paper: Sec. 3.2, App. B.1, Fig. 2

Start at the top: the videos were hand-picked from YouTube so that each one suits the tasks it will carry, and the running tour video lands in life record. The eight rows are the scenario categories of Sec. 3.2 with the sub-genres named in the paper's Figure 2; together they range from 3-second clips to 24-minute recordings, and the largest share of questions falls on videos under a minute.

The lower group shows how the 900 videos are divided among the three task categories: 500 videos with 2,500 questions for real-time visual understanding, 200 with 1,000 for omni-source understanding, and 200 with 800 evaluated questions for contextual understanding, of which only 50 are proactive output. Figure 4 follows the automatic route that produces the largest share of the questions.

Figure 4. GPT-4o generates timestamped questions from stamped frames or captions
Paper: Sec. 3.2, App. B.2, App. B.3, Fig. 6

This is the GPT-4o route of the paper's Figure 6, used for the ten real-time visual tasks and for proactive output. Frames are sampled once per second and each one gets its clock time printed in the top-left corner, which is what lets the generator place a question at a realistic moment such as 00:24 instead of at the end of the video. From there the route forks: tasks that need several frames go through a captioning step in which GPT-4o describes every block of 20 frames with time ranges in the [H:MM:SS - H:MM:SS] format, either for the whole video or for uniformly chosen clips; tasks that need a single frame skip captions, because the authors found that questions written straight from the stamped images came out better.

Question generation then combines the captions or frames with a task prompt that holds the task definition and a few worked examples, and asks for four-option questions phrased in the present or recent past. Each generated record carries the task type, question, timestamp, options and answer, plus a tag for the time range in which the clues appear; that tag later feeds the paper's analysis of prior, concurrent and subsequent clues. The caption line in the left box is illustrative: the paper prints the format but no caption for this video. Figure 5 shows the resulting record formats, including the hand-written ones.

Figure 5. What a multiple-choice, sequential QA and proactive output entry contains
Paper: Sec. 3.1.3, Sec. 4.1, App. A.2, App. B.2, Fig. 12; repo: src/data/questions_*.json

Each group is one record format from the released data; purple rows reach the model under test, grey rows stay with the grader. The first format serves 16 tasks: a question, a timestamp and four options, written by GPT-4o or by an annotator. The timestamp is not shown as text; it decides how much of the video the model gets. In the running example the model sees the tour up to 00:24 and has to pick UNIVERSAL STUDIOS HOLLYWOOD.

A sequential question answering video carries five questions that refer back to earlier ones; at evaluation time the earlier turns are supplied to the model together with their ground-truth answers, on the argument that a person in a real conversation would correct a wrong reply. The paper's example asks at 06:09 what has changed in the scene of the previous query, which was the balcony at 05:45. A proactive output entry replaces the options with a condition and a keyword; the model learns the keyword because the yes/no question names it, but the ground-truth time (08:24 here) is held back and only used for scoring. Figure 6 shows what happens to every record before release.

Figure 6. Quality control: answerability check, human verification, option shuffling
Paper: Sec. 3.1.2, Sec. 3.2, Fig. 6

All candidate pairs, generated or hand-written, pass the same checks. The first is whether the question could be answered from its wording alone; the paper's Figure 6 draws this as an automatic step, and the text lists it among the reasons for discarding. Human reviewers then read each pair for a correct label, a clear wording and a genuine tie to the streaming moment; for omni-source tasks they also make sure the question needs both the picture and the sound. The running example passes because its answer changes between 00:24 and 01:26.

Pairs leave the review on one of three paths: kept, revised when ambiguous or mislabelled, or discarded when the video is not needed. The options of surviving pairs are shuffled so that no answer letter dominates. The released pool is 4,500 questions on 900 videos; 200 further proactive output questions were written but held back from the current version. Figures 7 and 8 show how these records are run against a model.

Figure 7. Multiple-choice evaluation: prefix clip, letter answer, history for sequential QA
Paper: Sec. 4.1, App. A.1, App. A.2; repo: src/benchmark/StreamingBench.py, src/benchmark/StreamingBenchSQA.py

This is how the paper turns a streaming question into something an offline model can answer. The video is cut from its start to the question's timestamp and that prefix, with its audio, is the whole input; the model never sees what comes after. Frames are then taken from the prefix with each model's own official setting, for instance 64 frames for GPT-4o or one frame per second for Qwen2-VL on clips under five minutes. The prompt presents the question and four options and asks for a single letter; accuracy per task and overall is the metric.

The side box is the only difference for sequential question answering: the prompt is prefixed with the earlier turns of the same video, each with its timestamp, question, options and ground-truth answer, and tells the model not to confuse them with the current question. The appendix also reports a variant that keeps only the 60 seconds before the timestamp, which the released code exposes as a context-time setting. Figure 8 covers the one task that this prefix protocol cannot handle.

Figure 8. Proactive output evaluation: polled every second, correct within two seconds
Paper: Sec. 4.1, App. A.2, Fig. 12; repo: src/benchmark/StreamingBenchProactive.py, src/data/count.py

Proactive output asks the model to speak on its own when a condition is met, which offline models cannot do, so the paper polls them. From the query time onward the model is shown the clip so far and asked, once per second, whether it is now the right time to output the keyword; a 'no' advances the clock by one second along the loop on the right, a 'yes' triggers a second prompt that asks for the output itself, and that second is recorded as the model's output time. In the running example the model should say 'cartoon dogs' at 08:24.

An entry counts as solved only if the recorded time is within two seconds of the ground-truth time, so 08:22 to 08:26 is accepted here; the released statistics script additionally checks that the keyword appears in the reply. The paper describes the polling window as the query time plus every second from four seconds before to four seconds after the ground truth; the released code polls every second from the query time until four seconds after the ground truth and clips from the query time rather than from the start. Only 50 of the 250 written entries are evaluated in this version.

Evaluation

Accuracy
Multiple-choice questions answered with the option letter; the model sees the video only up to the question time.
Proactive output
From the question time on, the model is asked every second whether it is the right time to output. A question counts as correct when the output time is within 2 s of the ground-truth time; the task score is the mean over questions.
\[\text{correct}\iff |t_{\text{out}}-t_{\text{gt}}|<2\ \mathrm{s}\]
Sequential QA
Same accuracy; earlier questions of the sequence are given in the prompt together with their ground-truth answers.
Overall
Combines the real-time, omni-source and contextual questions; proactive output and sequential QA are reported separately.
