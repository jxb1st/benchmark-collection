VideoMemBench

Multi-turn conversations about one video where later turns depend on earlier ones: episodic dependencies refer to what was seen, answer dependencies refer to what the model said.

Statistics

  • 326 videos, one conversation each: 2,476 question turns in all, usually 8 per video (4 to 21).
  • 1,387 turns can only be answered by remembering earlier turns; 1,089 can be answered from the video alone.

Two kinds of memory (one turn can have several links)

  • Episodic1,200 linksthe turn points back to something seen earlier in the video
  • Answer594 linksthe turn points back to what the model itself answered earlier

Answer format: what the model has to produce

  • Open answer1,709 turnsfree text, judged against the reference answer
  • Multiple choice603 turns465 with 8 options, 51 with 4 options, 32 with 5 options, 31 with 6 options, 24 with 7 options
  • True or false164 turnsyes or no

Construction

Figure 1. From a video pool to a scored multi-turn memory benchmark
Paper: Sec. 3.2, Fig. 2, Sec. 4.1, Sec. 4.2

Read it top to bottom: the five gold boxes are the construction side, and the single purple box at the end is the only place where a model under test appears. The film recap enters as one of about 3.2k candidate videos, survives the labeler, and reaches the planner, which decides that this video gets nine turns over three distant scene clusters and that T3 will depend on T1's answer. The generator then writes T1, T2, T3 and so on one at a time, and every turn is sent to the judge; the lane on the right carries a rejected turn back to the generator together with the name of the failed check and the judge's reason, and two rejections in a row send the affected part of the plan back to the planner. Only dialogues that pass every turn-level check and still match the planned graph reach the eight experts.

The point to notice is the order of operations: the dependency structure is decided before any question is written, so a dependency is never an afterthought attached to a finished question. What the experts retain becomes the 326 dialogues that the evaluation protocol replays turn by turn. Figure 2 shows what one of those dialogues contains.

Figure 2. One sample: video, nine-turn dialogue, typed dependency graph
Paper: Sec. 1, Fig. 1, Sec. 3.1, Sec. 3.3, Fig. 2; repo docs/DATA_FORMAT.md

Start with the dependency graph in the middle. The nine turns are laid out in order; an arrow above the row is an episodic dependency and an arrow below the row is an answer dependency, and in both cases the arrowhead points at the later turn. T1 and T8 have no parents. T2 and T9 look back at the briefing-room scene of T1 and T5 returns to the moment of T4, while T3 and T6 need T1's answer, T4 needs T3's and T7 needs T5's. The same graph shows the two extra stresses the paper adds: T9 has two parents of different types, and the edge from T1 to T9 spans eight turns, so the first answer stays live until the end.

The three boxes spell out what the two edge types demand. T2 can be answered by anyone who remembers which scene T1 was about, even if T1 was answered wrongly; T3 cannot be decided without knowing that T1's answer was an anteater. Neither question repeats that content, which is what the three requirements at the bottom enforce: the parents must be necessary, the question must not leak what they established, and the answer must be visible in the video. Figure 3 starts the pipeline that produces such samples.

Figure 3. About 3.2k collected videos curated to 326 by a labeler LLM
Paper: Sec. 3.2, App. A, App. C (video curation), Fig. 3

The videos come from three kinds of sources: five existing benchmarks, the EgoLife recordings whose recurring people and activities make long-range dependencies possible, and web searches with a fixed list of queries for sports, talks, tutorials, film recaps and cooking. The labeler LLM sees uniformly sampled frames with time tags and returns one domain out of 31, a list of scenes with time windows, and a suitability verdict; a video with fewer than three visually distinct scenes, mostly slides, or content that cannot be verified from frames is dropped. The curated pool has 326 videos in four categories, and the duration row shows that about a quarter of them run over half an hour, the longest for 23.47 hours.

What matters for the next stage is that the scene list travels with the video: the planner in Figure 4 receives it together with the frames.

Figure 4. The planner fixes the dependency graph before any question exists
Paper: Sec. 3.2, App. B, App. C (Stage 1), Fig. 2

The planner is the only role that reasons about the whole video at once, and it writes no questions. Its output has four parts, read top to bottom: a synopsis of the entities and events, a suggestion of how many turns the dialogue has and how it moves through the video, the dependency graph with a parent list and an edge type for every dependent turn, and one intent per turn with its evidence window. For the film recap it chose nine turns across three scene clusters far apart in time, and for T1 an independent turn with evidence at 00:32-00:47 that establishes the suspect.

The rules group is where the two edge types are defined operationally: an answer edge is allowed only if a wrong parent answer would change the child's correct answer, and an episodic edge only needs the child to return to the parent's scene or entity. The graph must also keep T1 independent, make more than half of the turns dependent, include at least one turn with two or three parents and at least one edge that skips a turn, and place evidence windows in the beginning, middle and end of the video. The complete plan goes to the generator in Figure 5 and is used again by the plan fidelity check in Figure 7.

Figure 5. The generator writes each turn answer first, naming parents by id
Paper: Sec. 3.2, App. C (Stage 2), Fig. 2; repo examples/annotation.schema.json

The generator writes one turn at a time and sees three things: the plan entry for that turn, the turns already written, and frames from the planned evidence window. It works answer first: it picks a single fact that is clearly visible in the window, then writes a question that asks for it, then the options or the accepted paraphrases and a short justification naming the frames. For T3 the fact is the animal the fox and rabbit meet at the shipping-container yard, and the question refers to T1 only as the target from T1.

The rule that shapes every dependent question is that the answering model will later see the earlier questions but never the earlier reference answers, so a parent may only be named by its turn id and an episodic parent gets just enough visual anchors to be found again. If no fact in the window serves the intent, the generator reports a plan issue instead of guessing. The turn record at the bottom is what the judge in Figure 6 checks and what the released data keeps; the evidence field is shown in seconds, as the schema stores it.

Figure 6. Judge probes for necessity, non-leakage and grounding; failed turns regenerate
Paper: Sec. 3.2, App. C (Stage 3), Fig. 2

Each generated turn passes three independent probes by the judge LLM, and each probe is deliberately shown a different slice of the information. The necessity probe gets the frames and the dialogue with the parent turns removed and must answer or abstain; if it still answers T3 correctly with a confidence of at least 0.5, the dependency was decorative and the turn is rejected. The non-leakage check gets no video at all, only T3 next to T1 and T1's answer, and fails the turn if it repeats part of that answer or re-describes the briefing-room scene. The grounding check gets only the frames of the evidence window and must reach the reference answer by itself. The judge also rejects a question that duplicates an earlier one.

A rejected turn goes back to the generator with the name of the failed check and the judge's reason; the rewrite keeps the planned id, type, dependency and window, and the arrow over the top shows that the rewritten turn is judged again. A second rejection in a row sends the affected portion of the dialogue back to the planner. Dialogues whose turns all pass move to the dialogue-level check in Figure 7.

Figure 7. Plan fidelity check, expert review, and the released dataset
Paper: Sec. 3.2, Sec. 3.3, App. C (dialogue-level check), App. D.1, Fig. 3, Fig. 5

The plan fidelity check closes the loop with Figure 4: the judge reads the finished dialogue without the video, decides from each question text alone which earlier turns it really needs and of which type, and rejects the dialogue if this realized graph differs from the planned one by a missing, extra or retyped edge, or if a turn does not serve its intent. Dialogues that pass go to eight human experts, who replay the video in a web interface with the evidence window marked on the timeline, answer each question themselves before the reference answer is revealed, and verify answer correctness, temporal grounding, dependency validity, necessity and non-leakage; for a multi-parent turn such as T9 they also check that each parent is indispensable. A turn is marked correct, answer wrong, dependency not required, wording leaks a parent, or duplicate, and can be edited in place or removed.

The bottom group is the released dataset: 326 dialogues with 2,476 turns, of which 1,387 depend on earlier turns through 1,794 typed edges, 348 turns with two or three parents, dependency distances up to nine turns and chains up to depth seven. Figure 8 shows how a model is run through such a dialogue.

Figure 8. Self-rollout: the model's own earlier answers stay in the history
Paper: Sec. 4.1, Sec. 4.2, App. D.2; repo docs/PROTOCOL.md, inference/prompts.py, inference/scoring.py

Purple marks everything the model under test receives. At turn t its input is the fixed sequence of 128 timestamped frames, the earlier questions in dialogue order, its own earlier replies exactly as it produced them, and the current question with the instruction to put the final answer in a box; the gold row lists what it never gets, namely reference answers, dependency labels and evidence windows. The model answers at temperature 0 with up to 2,048 tokens, the reply is appended to the conversation, and the arrow over the top carries the grown history into the next turn. This is the self-rollout: a wrong answer at T1 is still in the context when T3 asks about the target from T1.

Grading is done per response and never changes the history. A multiple-choice reply is scored by extracting the option letter, an open reply is judged by GPT-5.4 mini for semantic equivalence with any accepted reference, and an option-less true/false reply by its last yes/no token; the grader sees the question, the references and the response, not the frames. The paper's human baseline follows the same protocol: twenty annotators, two per dialogue, see the same 128 frames and their own previous answers. An oracle variant used only for analysis replaces the model's earlier replies with reference answers. Figure 9 turns the per-turn verdicts into the three accuracies.

Figure 9. Three accuracies; answer-dependency credit requires a fully correct chain
Paper: Sec. 4.2 (Eq. 2); repo docs/PROTOCOL.md, inference/metrics.py, examples/README.md

The three metrics differ in which turns they pool and in whether earlier turns matter. Turn accuracy pools all turns and scores each on its own. Episodic dependency accuracy pools the turns with at least one episodic parent and also scores each on its own, because an episodic edge points at a scene the parent question described, not at the parent's answer. Answer dependency accuracy pools the turns with at least one answer parent and uses chain crediting: a turn earns credit only if it and every ancestor reachable by following answer edges backwards are correct, and episodic edges are never followed. Overall is the plain mean of the three, and a turn with both edge types counts in both dependency metrics.

The lower group applies this to the example dialogue under an illustrative outcome in which the model gets only T1 wrong. Turn accuracy is 8 of 9. T2, T5 and T9 have episodic parents and are all correct, so episodic dependency accuracy is 3 of 3 even though T1, the parent of T2 and T9, was wrong. Of the five answer-dependent turns, T3 and T6 lose their credit because T1 is their answer ancestor and T4 loses it through T3 and T1, while T7 keeps it because T5's own incoming edge is episodic and is not followed, and T9 keeps it because its only answer ancestor is T8; answer dependency accuracy is 2 of 5. This is the mechanism behind the paper's finding that answer-dependent turns show the largest gaps, and behind the oracle-history analysis that recovers much of that loss.

Step 2: Dialogue suggestion
Decide how many turns the dialogue should have (between {min_turns} and {max_turns}) and how

-turn_id (T1, T2, ...)

-"answer": the child cannot be answered without the parent's CORRECT answer.

"answer".

supplies information required by the child.
-"episodic": the child only needs to return to the same scene, moment, or entity as the parent.
The parent's answer being wrong does not change the child's answer.
-Parents must come earlier than the child. The graph must have no cycles.
-More than half of the turns must have at least one parent.
-At least one turn must have two or three parents.
-At least one edge must span two or more turns (not only adjacent turns).
-T1 must be independent and may use any supported question type.
-Evidence windows must cover the beginning, middle, and end of the video.
-Every edge must be genuine: the child's target must be hard to describe without the parent.
Do not connect turns that are about unrelated things.

Evaluation

Per turn
Each turn \(t\) is graded \(c(t)\in\{0,1\}\). Multiple choice and true/false by extracting the option; open answers by a judge model (GPT-5.4 mini) against the reference answers.
TurnAcc
Accuracy over all turns \(T\).
\[\mathrm{TurnAcc}=\frac{100}{|T|}\sum_{t\in T}c(t)\]
EpiDepAcc
Accuracy over the turns with an episodic dependency (the turn refers to something seen earlier); the parent turn need not be answered correctly.
\[\mathrm{EpiDepAcc}=\frac{100}{|T_{\mathrm{epi}}|}\sum_{t\in T_{\mathrm{epi}}}c(t)\]
AnsDepAcc
Accuracy over the turns with an answer dependency; a turn counts only if it and every earlier answer it builds on, \(\mathrm{anc}(t)\), are correct.
\[\mathrm{AnsDepAcc}=\frac{100}{|T_{\mathrm{ans}}|}\sum_{t\in T_{\mathrm{ans}}}c(t)\prod_{a\in\mathrm{anc}(t)}c(a)\]
Overall
Mean of the three.
\[\mathrm{Overall}=\frac{\mathrm{TurnAcc}+\mathrm{EpiDepAcc}+\mathrm{AnsDepAcc}}{3}\]

- score: either 0 or 1 (binary only, no fractional values)

- reason: a short explanation that mentions the key match or mismatch
You must always provide a numeric score.
