LVBench

Extreme long video understanding: 103 YouTube videos averaging 68 minutes, with multiple-choice questions tagged by six capabilities and a time reference for the evidence.

Construction

Figure 1. How LVBench goes from long YouTube videos to per-capability accuracy
Paper: Sec. 3.1, Sec. 3.2, Sec. 3.3, Sec. 3.4, Sec. 4.1, App. A.2

Read the main column top to bottom: the three construction steps the paper describes (collection, three-stage annotation, quality control) lead to the released set, and the purple box at the bottom is the only place a model under test appears; everything above it is the authors' and annotators' work. The box on the right is not a step but the design that shapes the annotation: six core capabilities that every video has to be questioned on and that can be combined on a single question. Following the running example: the 1:40:20 FA Cup match passes the screening, an annotator writes the question about the push and its four options, the pair survives the text-only filter, and at test time a model sees frames of the match with the question and answers with a letter.

The point to keep in mind for the later figures is the multi-tagging: one question can test reasoning, event understanding and entity recognition at the same time, so one correct letter later counts toward three capability accuracies. Figure 2 opens the first box.

Figure 2. Video collection: six categories, five hand-checked criteria, 103 long videos
Paper: Sec. 3.1, Sec. 5 (License), App. A.2, App. A.3, App. A.4, repository README and scripts/download.sh

Start with the upper group: the authors searched YouTube with keywords from six categories and let its search and recommendation results fill a pool of 500 candidates. The paper defines a long video as at least 30 minutes with several events and scene changes, and the annotators then screened the pool against the five criteria in the gold group; 103 videos with 117 hours in total survived. The running example is a full football match, so it has identifiable protagonists, a beginning-middle-end structure and an event every few minutes, and the last two criteria guarantee that it can be judged from the picture alone.

Two details are easy to miss. The modality-independence criterion is what lets the evaluation leave audio out without making questions unanswerable. And the videos themselves are not distributed: the released file holds one YouTube ID per video (field key) plus a download script built on video2dataset, under a CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence; the authors keep their own copies, which they hand out only with the creators' permission. The repository's cover image lists finer sub-categories (for example team sports and individual sports under Sports); the paper itself names only the six categories and the keyword examples drawn here. Figure 3 shows the capability taxonomy the annotators then had to cover on each video.

Figure 3. Six core capabilities, their shares, and how tags combine
Paper: Sec. 3.2, Fig. 1, Fig. 2, App. B, Fig. 5; released metadata for the tag counts

The upper group lists the six skills in the order of their share of the 1,549 questions (Fig. 2 of the paper); the shares add up to more than 100% because a question can carry several tags. Entity recognition and event understanding dominate, and the paper's own explanation uses the running example: asking why Player 4 pushed Player 17 is a reasoning question, but answering it means telling the two players apart by number and shirt colour (entity recognition) and seeing the push as an action in the match (event understanding). Reasoning at 12.9% and summarization at 3.7% are the rarest tags; the examples beside the other skills are further Fig. 1 questions on the same match.

The lower group shows what the combinations look like in the released file: 1,038 questions carry one tag, 477 two and 34 three, which gives the 26 fine-grained types of App. B; entity recognition alone is the most common type and entity recognition with event understanding the most common pair, matching the bar order of the paper's Fig. 5. The scoring consequence in the last box is what makes the tags matter: a correct answer to the push question is credited to three capability accuracies at once. Figure 4 shows how the annotators produced such questions.

Figure 4. Annotation in three stages: analysis, questions, answers with clue duration
Paper: Sec. 3.3, App. A.3, Fig. 1; released metadata for the time references

Start with the annotator box: the work was done by paid professional annotators on an annotation platform, at about 30 US dollars per video on average, and each one watches the whole video before writing anything. Stage 1 is note-taking: events, scene changes, the entities worth asking about, and the temporal or causal links between them; for the match this is where the push at 34:04 and its cause get marked. Stage 2 turns the notes into questions at about 24 per hour of video, spread across the whole duration, covering all six capabilities on every video, and worded so that the referent is unique: the paper prefers 'When did A and B start arguing?' over 'Why did they start arguing?', and the running example names both players by number and colour.

Stage 3 adds the four choices and a piece of annotation most video benchmarks do not have: the clue duration, the start and end of the shortest segment that suffices to answer. The distractors mirror the key in form and length and reuse plausible but wrong content from the video (an offensive move, retaliation, an accident). For the push question the released file gives 34:04-34:24, 20 seconds out of 100 minutes, while a summarization question on the same match spans 49:23-99:46. The paper prints no annotation guideline or form; the rules shown are its prose. The all-six rule applies to the drafts: in the released file, after the filtering of Figure 5, 25 of the 103 videos still carry all six tags. Figure 5 follows the draft pairs into quality control.

Figure 5. Quality control: fewer time-range questions and a two-model text-only filter
Paper: Sec. 3.4, Sec. 4.5, App. A.2

Two corrections in sequence. The first was made while annotation was running: annotators tended to pin a question's referent with a time range, which turns most questions into temporal grounding and favours models that can read timestamps, so they were told to keep referents unique by other means and to recast such questions as other types; the running example does this with jersey numbers and colours. The second came after all questions were written: GLM-4 and GPT-4 each answered every question from its text alone, and a pair was removed when both gave the same letter and that letter was the key. The paper gives no concrete removed question and no count of removals; it reports that the pass eliminated most text-answerable items and left 1,549 pairs.

The red box is the only use of that colour in the set. The filter is a model-assisted step on the annotation side, not an evaluation, which is why its box stays neutral rather than purple. The paper later checks the outcome with a zero-frame control (Sec. 4.5), where models given only the text score near chance, and reads this as evidence that the filter worked. Figure 6 shows the fields of a pair that was kept.

Figure 6. One released record and which fields the model sees
Paper: App. A.2, repository README, compute_accuracy.py and the released video_info.meta.jsonl

Purple rows reach the model under test: the video, fetched from its YouTube ID and shown as frames, and the question with its four options, which the file stores inside the question string as (A) to (D). Gold rows are held back for scoring: the answer letter, the list of capability tags, and the clue duration (time_reference). Neutral rows are metadata: the category key and the duration, frame rate and resolution recorded at download time. The running example is uid 3642 on a 100.34-minute, 25 fps, 1920 x 1080 video of category sport.

One discrepancy is worth knowing: the paper's Fig. 1 labels this question Reasoning, Event Understanding and Entity Recognition, but the released file tags it reasoning only; multi-tag questions do exist in the file, 511 of them. The field names follow the datasheet (App. A.2) and the official scoring code, which walks the qa list and matches on uid. Figure 7 shows how such a record is used at test time.

Figure 7. Evaluation: frames, one fixed prompt, letter extraction, accuracy per tag
Paper: Sec. 4.1, Sec. 4.3, Sec. 5 (Limitations), repository test_acc.py, compute_accuracy.py, answer_util.py

The top group is the model's input. Models with native long-video support get the video at one frame per second (about 6,020 frames for the 1:40:20 match), downsampled only when the video exceeds the model's limit; models built for images or short clips get a fixed number of frames such as 32 or 96 to match their training length. No audio is given, consistent with the modality-independence criterion of Figure 2. Every model receives the same prompt: the question, the four options labelled (A) to (D), and an instruction to return only the letter (verbatim in prompts.json).

Scoring is mechanical. The paper extracts the letter with a regular expression and falls back to a language model only when that fails; the repository's polish_answer takes the first character after an opening parenthesis, so '(C) ...' becomes C, and compute_accuracy compares it with the key. Sec. 4.3 shows why extraction matters: Gemini 1.5 Pro replied outside the options 20.9% of the time and two other models mostly chose A. A correct answer is counted once in the overall accuracy and once for every tag the question carries, so the push question feeds three capability columns; result.json holds those six numbers plus overall and is what the Hugging Face leaderboard takes.
