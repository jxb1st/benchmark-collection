Video-MME

Multiple-choice QA over YouTube videos of three length groups (under 2 min, 4 to 15 min, 30 to 60 min), 6 domains and 30 subcategories, with 12 task types; subtitles and audio are optional inputs.

  • 2,700 multiple-choice questions, three per video, four options each. 744 videos also have subtitles.

Construction

Paper: Sec. 3.1, Sec. 4.1, App. Sec. 7, Tab. 1

Read the boxes top to bottom. The three construction steps the paper names (video collection, question-answer annotation, quality review) produce the released set of 900 videos with 3 QA pairs each; the purple box at the bottom is the only place where a model under test appears, everything above it is the authors' and annotators' work. Following the running example: the road-trip vlog is collected together with its subtitles and audio track, an annotator writes the date question with four candidate dates, the review confirms that the date cannot be inferred from the question text alone, and at test time a model receives sampled frames (plus the subtitles in one of the two settings) and answers with a single letter that is compared with the key B.

The design point to notice is the arrow on the right: a QA pair that fails the review is sent back to the original annotator for revision rather than dropped, which is how the final set keeps exactly three questions for every video. Figure 2 opens the first box.

Paper: Sec. 3.1, Sec. 3.2, Fig. 2, Tab. 1

The hierarchy comes first: six domains chosen from what is popular on YouTube, each split into tags, 30 sub-categories in total (the upper group lists all of them). Every sub-category was then filled with videos of three lengths, so the three duration classes end up with 300 videos each; the averages in the three boxes are the per-class means of Tab. 1 (80.7 s, 515.9 s and 2,466.7 s). The running example sits in the long class; the paper does not print which sub-category it belongs to.

Two details are easy to miss. The classes are separated by gaps (nothing between 2 and 4 min or between 15 and 30 min), so short, medium and long are distinct regimes rather than a continuum. And the extra tracks were collected asymmetrically: the audio exists for all 900 videos, subtitles only where the uploader provided them (744 videos; the repository adds that every long video has them). Figure 3 shows what one collected entry contains.

Paper: Sec. 3.1, Sec. 3.2, Fig. 1, App. Sec. 7, repository output template

Purple rows are what reaches the model under test (the video as sampled frames, optionally the subtitles or the audio, the question and its four options); gold rows are the annotation that is held back for scoring (the key and the task-type label); neutral rows are the metadata used to break accuracy down by duration, domain and sub-category. In the running example the evidence for the key is spread over modalities and over half an hour of video: the start date and the departure day are only visible in frames at 01:10 and 31:16, the reason Yosemite matters comes from the spoken track, and the answer still needs the arithmetic from Day 1 to Day 10.

The field names follow the official results template of the repository (video_id, duration, domain, sub_category, and per question question_id, task_type, question, options, answer). The domain and sub-category shown for the vlog are an illustrative placement, see the provenance list. Figure 4 shows how the annotators wrote the question rows.

Paper: Sec. 3.1, Sec. 3.2, Fig. 2

Start with the annotator box: the authors recruited people with strong English and research experience in vision-language learning, and each one watches a video in full before writing, iteratively, three questions with four options each. The middle group lists the rules every pair has to satisfy; the running example meets the first one because both dates exist only in the frames, and its four options are all plausible dates. The paper gives these rules in prose, there is no printed annotation guideline or form.

The lower group is the label each question receives: one of 12 task types, seven perceptual, four reasoning, plus information synopsis. The distribution is not uniform across lengths: short videos carry mostly perception questions, long videos mostly reasoning questions, which the paper later uses to explain part of the accuracy drop with duration. The two examples for perception and synopsis come from video 001 of the repository's results template, not from the paper. Figure 5 follows the 2,700 draft pairs into the review.

Paper: Sec. 3.1, Sec. 3.2, Tab. 2, Tab. 3

Two gates in sequence. First a different annotator checks the wording, whether the question is answerable, whether the options are logically consistent and whether the marked key is right. Then the question text is given to Gemini 1.5 Pro without the video: anything the model can answer from the words alone is treated as not grounded in the video and goes back to the original annotator for revision. The red box holds the paper's own illustration of a rejected question (the biggest achievement of Argentina's number 10 in 2022, which any model resolves to the World Cup); the running example passes because no amount of world knowledge yields the departure date.

The bottom group collects the checks the paper reports on the finished set: Gemini 1.5 Pro stays below 15% accuracy on text alone, the four keys are close to uniform, question, option and answer lengths barely change across the three classes, and the certificate length (the minimal set of clips a verifier needs, borrowed from EgoSchema, measured on a sample of 3 videos per class) grows from a median of 26 s to 890.7 s. The paper gives neither the prompt used for the Gemini filter nor the number of pairs that were sent back. Figure 6 shows how a kept pair is used at test time.

Paper: Sec. 4.1, App. Sec. 7, repository README and eval_your_results.py

The top group is the model's input. Frames are sampled as each model's official configuration prescribes (Gemini 1.5 Pro at 1 fps, halved for long videos to keep the API stable; fixed counts elsewhere, e.g. 384 for GPT-4o, 10 for GPT-4V, 8 for Video-LLaVA); in the with-subtitles setting only the subtitle lines at the sampled frames' timestamps are added, so the text never carries more of the video than the frames do; audio is given only to models that accept it, in the paper Gemini 1.5 Pro. The question is wrapped in the model's own multiple-choice prompt when it has one, otherwise in the standardized prompt reproduced in prompts.json, which asks for only the letter.

Scoring is deliberately mechanical: the official script strips answer prefixes such as 'The best answer is', takes the first A to D it finds and compares it with the key, with no ChatGPT-style judge. Two details from the script matter when reading numbers: a response with no letter is not counted as answered (accuracy is correct over answered), and the script insists on exactly 300 videos per duration class. Accuracy is then broken down by duration, domain, sub-category and task type, separately for the two settings.

Evaluation

Accuracy
Four-option multiple choice; the letter is extracted from the reply with a regular expression, no judge model.
Two settings
Without subtitles, and with the subtitles that belong to the sampled frames.
Reported by
Short, medium and long videos, and overall.
