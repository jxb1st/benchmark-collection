OVO-Bench

Online video understanding in three modes: backward tracing, real-time visual perception, and forward active responding (the model must wait, count or follow steps as the stream continues). Twelve tasks.

Statistics

  • 644 videos from twelve source datasets; Ego4D is the largest public one (523 records).
  • 1,640 annotation records with 3,035 questions. In the three forward tasks the same question is asked again several times as the video goes on, so one record holds several questions.
  • A question is asked at a point in time, 1:56 into the video on median. The model sees only the video up to that point.

Answer format: what the model has to produce

  • Multiple choice1,468 questionsbackward tracing and real-time perception; 1,371 with 4 options, 42 with 2 options, 42 with 3 options, 13 with 5 options
  • A number698 questionsrepetition event count: the count so far at each query
  • Yes or No869 questionsstep recognition (629 queries: is this step being performed now) and clue reveal (240: can the question be answered yet)

Construction

Figure 1. How OVO-Bench is built, from taxonomy to timeline evaluation
Paper: Sec. 3, Fig. 3, Sec. 4.1

Read the column top to bottom. The authors first fix what an online model should be able to do (three modes, twelve tasks), then pick, task by task, a public dataset whose annotations fit, and widen the domains with videos crawled from YouTube. Every item then needs an event-level timestamp, which is what separates this benchmark from offline ones: the two gold boxes are where volunteers, the authors and the annotation models (Gemini-1.5, GPT-4o and other Video-LLMs) produce and check those timestamps, questions and options.

The running example is one YouTube ice-hockey broadcast. Its meta-annotation records that the text LAFFONT, 00 is readable on a jacket at 7:46; the option step turns that into a four-way question whose distractors are other jerseys from the same video; the evaluation step (purple) shows the model only the clip from 0:00 to 7:46 and expects the letter A. The design point is that the query time belongs to the item: the same question asked earlier on the same video has a different correct answer. Figure 2 opens the first box.

Figure 2. Three online modes and the twelve tasks under them
Paper: Sec. 3.1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3

Look at the right-hand note of each dashed block first: it gives the paper's definition of the mode as the part of the stream, relative to the query time t0, that holds the clue. Backward tracing looks before a recency threshold T, real-time visual perception looks at the latest frames, and forward active responding must wait for frames that have not arrived yet. The first two modes are answered at once as multiple choice; the third is open-ended and is scored on when the model answers.

Each row is one task with its abbreviation spelled out and a question taken from the paper's examples, some shortened to fit the row. The running example sits in the OCR row (optical character recognition) under real-time perception. Hallucination detection (HLD) is the odd one out: its question asks about something the stream has not shown yet, so the right choice is the escape option "Unable to answer". Figure 3 shows where the videos for each task come from.

Figure 3. Which public dataset or web source feeds each task
Paper: Sec. 3.2.1, Sec. 3.3, Fig. 4, App. 11, Tab. 2

The upper block lists, per task, the human-annotated datasets whose existing labels match the task; only validation or test splits are used, to limit leakage from model training data. Episodic memory and hallucination detection draw on egocentric and embodied question-answering sets, action sequence identification on instructional-video sets with ordered steps, repetition counting on datasets with counted actions, and clue-revealing questions on films. Ego4D supplies the egocentric footage for the real-time tasks.

The lower block is the second source: videos crawled from YouTube to widen the domains (sports, games, vlogs); the ice-hockey broadcast of the running example belongs here. Together this gives 644 videos from a few minutes to half an hour long, about a third tutorials and a third egocentric footage. The paper names the sources of HLD only through its appendix examples (QA-Ego4D and Open-EQA), which the row says. Figure 4 shows how these videos receive event-level timestamps.

Figure 4. Four routes to event-level timestamps, then human checking
Paper: Sec. 3.2.1, Sec. 3.2.2, App. 7.1

The dashed block lists the ways an item gets its event-level timestamp, the piece of information the whole evaluation depends on. Where a source dataset already has accurate event times (QA-Ego4D, COIN, Ego4D) they are kept and rebuilt into the final item. Where a dataset has only video-level question-answer pairs (OpenEQA, STAR, Perception Test, THUMOS), Gemini-1.5 is asked to localize the referred event coarsely. For the real-time tasks the timestamp falls out of the question-construction step itself: a short clip is sampled from the long video and its position becomes the query time. For SSR and CRR, volunteers write question, answer and times by hand, because even strong Video-LLMs could not produce usable clue-revealing questions.

All routes pass through the same gold box: coarse model timestamps are corrected by people, and every source video and meta-annotation is inspected. For the running example the output is a record saying that in the hockey video the jacket text LAFFONT, 00 is visible at 7:46. The prompts given to Gemini-1.5 for this step are not printed in the paper. Figure 5 turns such records into questions with options.

Figure 5. Question sources, present-tense rewrite and visually grounded options
Paper: Sec. 3.2.2, App. 7.1, App. Fig. 9

Start at the top row: a question-answer pair comes from one of three places. Some are selected from the source datasets' own QA; many real-time items are written by GPT-4o from randomly sampled short clips, using prompts the authors refined by hand; and volunteers add questions for spatial understanding, object recognition, attribute recognition and all of CRR, to counter the preferences of a single generator model. Real-time questions are then rephrased by hand so that they read as live queries ("What am I doing ...", "now", "currently").

The lower block is the option step for the nine multiple-choice tasks. Rather than free-form distractors, the correct answer is transformed by rules into options grounded in the same video, with a Video-LLM drafting them from the clip and the QA pair, so that wrong options describe things that really appear (for the jacket question: the other three jerseys seen earlier). Reviewers check that options are relevant, misleading and of similar length, then shuffle them; items end up with two to five options. Two special cases sit below the block: HLD items carry "Unable to answer" as the correct option because their clue arrives only after the query, and the three forward tasks have no options at all.

The GPT-4o generation prompt and the option-generation prompt are not printed in the paper; the volunteer guidelines are (App. 7.1). Figure 6 shows how a finished item is put to a model.

Figure 6. Evaluation on a clip cut at the query time
Paper: Sec. 4.1, Tab. 1 caption, App. 6.3, App. Fig. 9; repository utils/chunk_videos.py, constant.py

Follow the column: an item carries its query time t; the video is physically cut to 0:00-t (the released code rounds t up to the next second and writes one clip per item); the clip is sampled at 64 uniform frames or 1 fps depending on the model; and the prompt gives the question with lettered options and asks for the letter alone. Scoring is a letter match, averaged per task and then over tasks.

The dashed block is the design point. The jacket question exists three times on the same video with query times 1:43, 5:09 and 7:46, and the correct jersey differs each time, so a model that ignores when it is asked and simply retrieves any matching frame cannot get all three right. Two placement rules complete the picture: for episodic memory and action sequence identification the query is put at the end of the video to maximize the distance to the clue, and for hallucination detection the clue lies after the query, so "Unable to answer" is correct. Figure 7 shows the different protocol used for the forward tasks.

Figure 7. Multiple triggering: the same query re-asked on growing clips
Paper: Sec. 3.2.2, Sec. 4.4, Fig. 7, App. 6.1, App. 6.2, App. Fig. 8; repository utils/OVOBench.py, utils/OVOBenchScore.py

Forward tasks cannot be answered on a single clip, because the information arrives after the query. The protocol therefore re-asks the same query at several trigger times t_i, each time on the clip 0:00-t_i, and every call is independent, with no memory of the previous answer. The example follows the tutorial "Make Sugar Coated Haws": for the step "melt the sugar" (1:22-1:46) the model is asked at 1:17, 1:20, 1:24, 1:27 and 1:46 whether that step is being performed now, and the expected answers are No, No, Yes, Yes, Yes.

The middle block shows the prompt form of each task: REC (repetition event count) asks for the running count as an integer, SSR (sequential steps recognition) asks per step for Yes or No, and CRR (clues reveal responding) asks whether the clip already contains enough information to answer. Accuracy over triggers is what the released code computes; the paper additionally defines a score that halves the credit as the answer comes later than the reference time (p = 0.5 for SSR and CRR; p1 = 0.2 and p2 = 0.05 for REC, where higher counts are rewarded), with GPT-4o judging SSR and CRR answers in that variant. The bottom block shows where the triggers sit for the paper's three examples: REC after each repetition, SSR and CRR shortly before and after the step or the clue.

Because an offline model is only ever asked whether there is enough information now, it can score above zero by always answering Yes; the paper lists this as a limitation of the protocol.

Illustrative in the figures, not from the paper: That the jacket question came out of the GPT-4o route and that its three distractors came from the model-drafted option step is inferred from the pipeline description (OCR is not among the tasks with volunteer questions, and the other jerseys are the answers of the sibling items); the paper does not state it for this item.; "COIN steps for ASI" as an instance of dataset QA pairs is composed from the source list in Sec. 3.2.1.; Several questions are shortened in the figures to fit a row, for instance "What name and number are on the jacket?".; The top-to-bottom order of Figure 1 follows the paper's section order; the paper does not say that the taxonomy was fixed before the sources were chosen..

Volunteer guidelines for real-time questions (STU, OJR, ATR) App. 7.1 (Sec. 7.1 of the supplement); Instructions given to volunteers who propose candidate questions and options for spatial understanding, object recognition and attribute recognition, to supplement the automatic GPT-4o pipeline.
• Watch the video and decide whether this candidate is appropriate for constructing questions that can be classified into the above three types.
• Selected appropriate moments for problem construction. Consider whether the moment contains: 1. Obvious spatial relationships between several objects; 2. Interested objects, such as something that appears in the moment, and so on; 3. Objects with unusual attributes, e.g. green fire, smooth woods.
• Construct options for the questions. Ensure that 1. Options should be relevant to the visual content; 2. Incorrect options should bring misleading information from the visual content; 3. Options should be as close in length as possible.
Volunteer guidelines for clues reveal responding (CRR) App. 7.1 (Sec. 7.1 of the supplement); Instructions given to volunteers who write the CRR queries, answers and clue-revealing times, after prompting Video-LLMs and LLMs for this task had failed.
• Find scenes with apparent discontinuity. For example, character A performs a certain action at query time Qi. However, the action’s complete process or outcome is not immediately shown during query time.
• Continue watching the video, find clues for your query, and annotate the clues revealing time as Ai.
• Try to provide concise timestamps, let Ai be the time when enough visual information has just been revealed.

Evaluation

Multiple-choice tasks
Backward tracing and real-time perception: a query is correct if the ground-truth answer appears in the reply.
Repetition event count (REC)
At each query, correct if the number in the reply equals the count so far.
Step recognition (SSR) and clue reveal (CRR)
At each query the model answers Yes or No; correct if it matches the expected answer.
Aggregation
A task is the mean over its queries (\(F_q = 1\) if query \(q\) is correct), a mode is the mean of its tasks, and Overall is the mean of the three modes.
\[\mathrm{Acc}_{\text{task}}=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}F_q,\qquad \mathrm{Overall}=\frac{\text{Backward}+\text{Real-time}+\text{Forward}}{3}\]
