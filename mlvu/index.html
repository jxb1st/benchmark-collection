MLVU

Multi-task long video understanding: nine dev tasks (seven multiple-choice, two generation) over movies, documentaries, egocentric, surveillance, game and cartoon videos from 3 minutes to 2 hours.

Statistics

  • Dev set: 2,593 questions. The videos are 3:00 to 9:02:30 long (median 8:00).
  • Seven tasks are multiple choice. Two (sub-scene captioning, video summary) ask for free text, which is scored against a reference answer.
  • The paper counts 1,730 videos and 3,102 questions for dev and test together. The test set is gated.

Answer format: what the model has to produce

  • Multiple choice2,175 questions2,175 with 4 options
  • Free text418 questionssub-scene captioning (201) and video summary (217), scored 0 to 10 against the reference

  • Plot QA539 questionsmultiple choice; 160 videos, mean 7:46
  • Needle QA355 questionsmultiple choice; 129 videos, mean 14:18
  • Ego reasoning352 questionsmultiple choice; 84 videos, mean 9:36

Construction

Figure 1. How MLVU was built: collection, creation routes, task set, scoring
Paper: Sec. 1, Sec. 3.1-3.2, App. C, App. E, App. G.3

Read the figure top to bottom. Everything starts from the Universal Long Video Collection (ULVC), a pool of 986 long videos gathered from public datasets and the authors' own collecting, spanning ten genres and lengths from three minutes to more than two hours. The running example is one of its movies: a roughly 14-minute clip taken from Movie101.

The middle band is the design point: questions reach the benchmark by three different routes. Four tasks (Topic Reasoning, Video Summarization, Plot Question-Answering, Sub-Scene Captioning) are written by human annotators under task guidelines; two (Anomaly Recognition, Ego Reasoning) reformat labels that UCF-Crime and Ego4D already provide; three (Needle Question-Answering, Action Order, Action Count) are synthesized by cutting short clips from other datasets into a long background video. The running example follows the manual route and becomes a Sub-Scene Captioning item.

The result is a set of 3,102 questions over nine tasks, split into a dev set (2,593 questions, four options each) and a test set (509 questions, six options). Models are evaluated zero-shot: they receive the video and a fixed prompt, multiple-choice answers are scored by accuracy, and the two generation tasks are scored by GPT-4. Figure 2 opens the first box.

Figure 2. Universal Long Video Collection: sources, genres, durations and segments
Paper: Sec. 1, Sec. 3.1, Fig. 1, Tab. 1, App. C, App. D

The upper panel lists the ten genres of the ULVC with their counts. Movies, documentaries, game videos, surveillance footage and egocentric videos come from Movie101, MovieChat, MineDojo, UCF-Crime and Ego4D; cartoons, TV series, tutorials, sports and life records were collected by the authors. The paper treats cartoons and games as simulated video and the rest as real-world footage; the sub-genres in the example lines are a selection of the labels in the paper's genre chart.

The lower panel covers length. Videos run from 3 minutes to over 2 hours and the mean benchmark video is about 15 minutes. Most tasks are annotated per segment: the first 3 minutes, the first 6 minutes and the whole video each get their own question, which is what allows the Time-ladder variant at 180, 360 and 600 seconds without new annotation. One source video can serve several tasks, whole, cut into clips, or as the background of a synthetic item, so the 986 collected videos become 1,730 benchmark videos. The running example is a 14-minute movie clip used for Sub-Scene Captioning. Figure 3 shows the tasks these videos feed.

Figure 3. Nine tasks in three categories, with format, count and origin
Paper: Sec. 3.2, Fig. 1, Fig. 2, App. E (Tab. 1)

Each dashed group is one of the three categories: holistic tasks need the whole video, single-detail tasks hinge on one plot or clip, and multi-detail tasks combine several clips. Within a group the rows are split by answer format, because the format decides the scorer: multiple choice is scored by accuracy, generation by GPT-4. The number at the start of each example line is the task's question count from the appendix table, followed by its origin: manual writing, reused labels, or inserted needle and probe clips.

The running example sits in the single-detail group as one of the 247 Sub-Scene Captioning items; its prompt asks how the man in red pants saved the woman. Seven of the nine tasks are multiple choice and only Video Summarization and Sub-Scene Captioning are free-form, which is why the evaluation protocol has two halves (Figures 7 and 8). Figure 4 follows the manual route first.

Figure 4. Manual annotation of four tasks, shown on the running example
Paper: Sec. 3.2.1-3.2.2, App. D, App. F.1, F.3, F.6, F.7 (App. Figs. 1, 2, 4, 6)

The upper group is the annotator's work for the four manually written tasks. The annotator watches the assigned material, writes the question or prompt, writes the answer and, for Sub-Scene Captioning only, breaks the caption into scoring points. The running example passes through all four boxes: the prompt refers to the man in red pants and the woman in the black and white striped dress rather than to named actors, the caption describes the rescue, and three scoring points (hooking a tree with one foot, grabbing her with his hand, preventing the fall) are what GPT-4 will later check.

The lower group lists what each task's guidelines ask for. Topic Reasoning wants a question about the genre, main event, setting or period with one correct and three plausible wrong options; Video Summarization wants a chronological, visual-only summary, additionally delineated at the first 3 and 6 minutes; Plot Question-Answering wants factual or inferential questions about a plot event that occurs only once in the video; Sub-Scene Captioning wants a prompt with a unique clue, a caption of a scene of about a minute at most, and the scoring points. For Plot Question-Answering and Sub-Scene Captioning the annotators also mark where in the long video the answer lies. The design point shared by all four is the pronoun rule: characters are described by attire, age or role, never by name, so a model cannot answer from a remembered plot. Figure 5 covers the five tasks that need no new writing.

Figure 5. Reused labels and synthetic needle or probe insertion
Paper: Sec. 3.2.1-3.2.3, App. D, App. F.2, F.4, F.5, F.8, F.9 (App. Fig. 3)

The top group holds the two tasks that reuse existing annotation. Anomaly Recognition takes UCF-Crime surveillance clips longer than three minutes and turns the dataset's anomaly labels into four-way options; Ego Reasoning takes queries and answers from the Natural Language Queries task of Ego4D and recasts them as multiple choice, keeping the answer intervals for the Time-ladder.

The lower group is the synthetic route. A short needle or probe clip (needles from WebVid and Clevrer, probes from Kinetics or from consecutive ActivityNet-Captions clips) is cut into a background video drawn at random from the ULVC. For Needle Question-Answering one needle is inserted and GPT-4 writes one to three clue-rich multiple-choice questions from the WebVid caption of the needle, using the prompt in the paper's appendix; the paper's example asks what the man on the lake shore is doing, with a polar-bear video as background. For Action Order several probes of different actions are inserted and the model must recover their order among wrong orders written by the annotators; for Action Count several probes of one action are inserted and the model counts them, for instance four 'carving a pumpkin' clips in a sitcom episode. The design point is the check on the background: the authors verify by hand that the needle scene or probe action does not already occur there, otherwise the count or the location would be ambiguous. Figure 6 collects this and the other controls.

Figure 6. Quality control rules against shortcuts and ambiguous references
Paper: Sec. 3.2, App. B, App. F.1-F.9

This table gathers the rules the paper applies so that questions can be answered only from the video. The first row shapes all written text: characters are referred to by pronouns or attributes ('the man in red pants'), never by name, in Plot Question-Answering, Video Summarization and Sub-Scene Captioning, so familiarity with a film's plot does not help. The next three rows protect the single- and multi-detail tasks: a referring clue must match exactly one scene, the background video is checked not to contain the inserted scene or action, and probe actions are chosen to be unusual in films (making jewelry, water skiing).

The remaining rows cover the rest of the design: summaries and questions rely on visual content only, Topic Reasoning questions must be about the main subject rather than minor details, each dev question has one correct option and three plausible distractors, and the test set is made harder with six options and withheld answers. The paper reports no inter-annotator agreement or rejection statistics, so no such numbers appear here. Figure 7 turns to how the finished questions are posed to a model.

Figure 7. Multiple-choice protocol: prompt template, letter match, accuracy
Paper: Sec. 3.2, App. B, App. G.1-G.3 (App. Fig. 7); repo evaluation/multiple_choice_evaluation/choice_bench.py

Follow the purple boxes: they are what the model under test receives or produces. The prompt is a short system line (one wording for video models, another for image models fed with frames), the question with its lettered options, and the instruction to give only the best option. The assistant turn is pre-filled with 'Best Option: (' so that the model's first characters are the letter; the example is the paper's Topic Reasoning question about the person in the game, answered with option B. The evaluation code takes the text before the first ')' as the predicted letter and compares it with the key.

Accuracy is computed per task on a 0 to 100 scale and M-Avg is the mean over the seven multiple-choice tasks; chance is 25 on the four-option dev set and 16.7 on the six-option test set. The lower group records the input conditions: frames are sampled by each model's own method (a fixed number of uniform frames or a frame rate), models run zero-shot with their official code or API, and the two system lines quoted are the ones printed in the paper's appendix. Figure 8 shows the other half of the protocol, where no letter can be matched.

Figure 8. Generation tasks scored by a GPT-4 judge with per-task criteria
Paper: Sec. 3.2.1-3.2.2, App. F.7, App. G.3 (App. Fig. 8); repo evaluation/generation_evaluation/

For the two generation tasks the model (purple) answers a fixed request, and the annotators' reference (gold) is either the standard summary or, for Sub-Scene Captioning, the scoring points from Figure 4. Both go to a GPT-4 judge (gpt-4-turbo at temperature 0 in the released code) whose system prompt holds the criteria and asks for a rationale followed by a JSON dictionary of scores. For the running example the judge checks the three scoring points about the tree, the grab and the prevented fall against the model's caption; the model answer shown in the figure is illustrative.

The criteria are task-specific, each on a 1 to 5 scale: Sub-Scene Captioning gets an accuracy score (1, 3 or 5 per scoring point, averaged) and a relevance score; Video Summarization gets completeness and reliability. The two scores are summed, so a task total lies on a 10-point scale, and G-Avg is the mean of the two task totals. The design point is that the captioning accuracy is anchored to the annotators' scoring points rather than to a holistic impression, which is why Figure 4 had the annotators split the caption in the first place.

Prompts the authors used to build it (5)
Annotation Guidelines for Topic Reasoning App. F.1, Fig. 1; Instructions given to human annotators who write the Topic Reasoning multiple-choice questions and answers.
1. Task Description: Your task is to formulate a question that pertains to the genre and key content
of a given long video, and then provide the corresponding answer.
2. Question Requirements:
- Your questions should be centered around the core content of the video, rather than focusing on
minor details.
- Suitable topics for questions include the genre of the video, the main events or themes, the
primary environmental setting, the depicted weather conditions, and the time period or timeline.
3. Question Format:
- Questions should be structured in a multiple-choice format. Each question should have one
correct answer and three plausible, yet incorrect, distractor options.
4. Question Examples (for reference only, not limited):
- What genre does this movie/video fall into?
- Where does the main scene in the video take place?
- What is the main event being narrated in the video?
- What is the protagonist in the video accomplishing?
Annotation Guidelines for Video Summarization App. F.3, Fig. 2; Instructions given to human annotators who write the reference summaries for Video Summarization.
1. Task Description: Your task is to provide a comprehensive summary of the key events occurring within a
video clip that ranges from 3 to 15 minutes in length.
2. Annotation Requirements:
- The annotation should encapsulate the principal events portrayed in the video, structured in chronological
order.
- Refrain from using specific character names in the annotation. Instead, all characters should be referred to
using pronouns and identified by their unique attributes or roles, such as attire, age, profession, etc. For
instance, characters could be described as an "elderly individual" (age), a "medical professional"
(profession), among others.
- Disregard audio-related information, such as dialogues between characters. The summaries should be
derived exclusively from the visual content presented in the video.
3. Annotation Template:
- Initiate your summary by outlining the overall content of the video: the event being narrated or the video's
main theme.
- Subsequently, chronologically depict the key events that unfold in the video. The aim is to provide a clear
and concise description of the main content, events, and scenes exhibited in the video.
4. Annotation Examples:
- Cartoon: This is a video about a cartoon sponge‘s whimsical adventures. The video begins with a cartoon
sponge rushing into a house to converse with a cartoon starfish on a rocking chair. The sponge then heads
to a concert hall where he watches a performance, during which a cartoon animal on a throne reprimands a
cartoon octopus who continues his act. Later, the cartoon sponge and a cartoon squirrel are seen flying and
conversing in the air. The sponge also encounters a cartoon shark preparing to drink coffee and a cartoon
lobster sailing on a sponge, after which the lobster chases the sponge away.
- Movie / TV Series: This is a video depicting a dramatic narrative. The video starts with a man singing into
a microphone, with a few other men playing instruments behind him. The scene changes to someone
pushing open a door and walking into a room where others are resting. She then opens another door, enters
a room and starts arguing with the singing man, which results in a fight. Next, the woman drives the man
away, which results in a car crash. The car then falls off a bridge and gets hit by another car. The screen
goes black and then lights up again, revealing a bookshelf filled with books at the end.
- Documentary: This is a documentary about forest animals and ecology. The video begins by showing
scenes of fish, butterflies, orangutans, and birds in the forest. Then, the video depicts two birds
cooperatively building a nest on a rock. As it starts to rain in the forest, a hatchling is born. The two birds
catch bugs and frogs in the forest and feed them to the newborn. The camera follows the direction of the
flowing river, which converges to form a spectacular waterfall. The video ends with a calm sea and beach,
with a large flock of seabirds flying over the sea, hunting for prey close to the water.

Annotation Guidelines for Plot Question-Answering App. F.6, Fig. 4; Instructions given to human annotators who write the Plot Question-Answering multiple-choice questions.
1. Task Description: Your task is to generate questions and answers based on the plot events depicted in
various media, including movie, TV series, and cartoon animations.
2. Question Requirements:
- The questions should target specific details or events within the given video. Both factual and inferential
questions are encouraged.
- Avoid using specific character names in the questions. Instead, use pronouns or identify characters by
unique attributes or roles (e.g., attire, age, profession).
- Ensure that the plot referred to in your question is unique within the long video. Avoid using vague
descriptions that can apply to multiple instances (like "eating"). Instead, refer to unique scenes or add
enough details to specify the exact event.
3. Question Format:
- Questions should be structured in a multiple-choice format. Each question should have one correct answer
and three plausible, yet incorrect, distractor options.
4. Examples of Questions (for reference only, not limited):
- How does the character in the small boat end up?
- How did the warship and the small boat approach each other?
- Why didn't the old man buy the chicken?
- What mode of transportation did the old man take in the end?
- What was the young woman doing when she drove to the airport?
Annotation Guidelines for Sub-Scene Captioning App. F.7, Fig. 6; Instructions given to human annotators who write the referring prompt, the caption and the scoring points of Sub-Scene Captioning.
1. Task Description: You are required to provide a detailed caption for a specific scene in a long movie and
clearly provide a unique prompt that can point to this scene.
2. Prompt Requirements:
- The clue in the prompt should direct to a specific and singular scene in the movie.
- Ensure that the prompt does not contain specific character names or movie-specific terms.
- The scene to be described should generally not exceed 1 minute.
3. Caption Requirements:
- Avoid using specific character names in the captions. Instead, use pronouns or identify characters by unique
attributes or roles (e.g., attire, age, profession).
- Provide a caption and a list of unique plot details as scoring points, ensuring there's no repetition of details
already present in the prompt.
4. Examples:
- Example (1):
- Prompt: Please describe the situation after the man at the door takes off his hat and throws it away.
- Caption: The hat flies into the room and is kicked into the large clock by the man in black who stands up.
- Scoring points: "The hat flies into the room", "is kicked into the large clock", "by the man in black who
stands up”
- Example (2):
- Prompt: Please describe the reaction of the short-haired man when the long-haired man took out the urn.
- Caption: The short-haired man stood up, held the urn in his hands, and pressed his forehead against the
mouth of the urn, unable to hold back his tears.
- Scoring points: "The short-haired man stood up", "held the urn in his hands", "pressed his forehead
against the mouth of the urn", "unable to hold back his tears"

Evaluation

M-Avg
Mean accuracy over the multiple-choice tasks \(\mathcal{M}\).
\[\text{M-Avg}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{Acc}_m\]
G-Avg
Mean of the two generation tasks, each scored by GPT-4 against the reference on a 0 to 10 scale: sub-scene captioning (SSC) on accuracy and relevance, video summarization (VS) on completeness and reliability.
\[\text{G-Avg}=\frac{\mathrm{SSC}+\mathrm{VS}}{2}\in[0,10]\]

##ACCURACY Scoring Criteria:
Evaluate the respondent's answer against specific scoring points as follows:
Score 1: The response completely misses the scoring point.
Score 3: The response mentions content related to the scoring point but is not entirely correct.
Score 5: The response accurately addresses the scoring point.
Calculate the average score across all scoring points to determine the final accuracy score.
##RELEVANCE Scoring Criteria:

Score 2: The response is partially related to the question but contains a significant amount of irrelevant content.
Score 3: The response primarily addresses the question, but the respondent seems uncertain about their own answer.
Score 4: The response mostly addresses the question and the respondent appears confident in their answer.
Score 5: The response is fully focused on addressing the question with no irrelevant content and demonstrates complete certainty.

##TASK DESCRIPTION:
You are required to evaluate the performance of the respondent in the video summarization task based on the standard answer and the

##COMPLETENESS Scoring Criteria:
The completeness score focuses on whether the summary covers all key points and main information from the video.
Score 1: The summary hardly covers any of the main content or key points of the video.
Score 2: The summary covers some of the main content and key points but misses many.
Score 3: The summary covers most of the main content and key points.
Score 4: The summary is very comprehensive, covering most to nearly all of the main content and key points.
Score 5: The summary completely covers all the main content and key points of the video.
##CORRECTNESS Scoring Criteria:

long as these details do not conflict with the correct answer and are reasonable, points should not be deducted.
Score 1: Contains multiple factual errors and contradictions; presentation is confusing.
Score 2: Includes several errors and some contradictions; needs clearer presentation.
Score 3: Generally accurate with minor errors; minimal contradictions; reasonably clear presentation.
Score 4: Very accurate with negligible inaccuracies; no contradictions; clear and fluent presentation.
Score 5: Completely accurate with no errors or contradictions; presentation is clear and easy to understand.
